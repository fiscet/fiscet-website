I keep running into the same story from different angles: AI's real bottleneck isn't compute, it's power, and specifically the years-long wait to connect a new data center to the grid. A Boston startup called Atomarine has a genuinely strange answer to that wait: stop trying to plug in at all, and put the data center on a boat.

Atomarine, founded by two MIT graduates (one with a PhD in nuclear engineering, the other with degrees spanning electrical engineering, mechanical engineering and business), builds floating, modular data center barges. The plan is to construct them in shipyards, tow them ten to twelve nautical miles offshore, and run them fully off-grid, anchored next to a separate power-delivery ship. Each platform is designed to deliver 75 to 100 megawatts.

The clever part is what building at sea sidesteps rather than what it adds. A data center on land needs grid interconnection, which now takes two to seven years in many regions, plus it needs to win over a community that doesn't want a power-hungry building next door. A barge needs neither: cooling comes free from the surrounding seawater instead of chillers, and the whole platform can reportedly be deployed up to four times faster than an equivalent onshore build.

Today the power ship burns LNG, so this isn't the zero-carbon story the "nuclear-powered" headlines imply, at least not yet. The real bet is on small modular reactors: once SMRs are certified for shipboard use, Atomarine wants to swap the gas-fired power ship for a nuclear one without touching the data center barge itself, since the two are physically separate vessels.

What I like is the sequencing. Solve deployment speed first with proven marine engineering, leave the harder nuclear-certification problem for later, and design the architecture so that swap never requires redesigning anything else. Betting compute infrastructure on shipyards rather than utilities is an odd wager. It might also be the right one.
